A decisional assumption can directly supply a generator: its real experiment
already compares a deterministic output with an appropriate uniform object.

* Construction

Let \(\mathbb G=\langle g\rangle\) be a public cyclic group of prime order
\(q\). Define

\[
G_{\mathrm{DH}}:\mathbb Z_q^2\longrightarrow\mathbb G^3,
\qquad G_{\mathrm{DH}}(a,b)=(g^a,g^b,g^{ab}).
\]

The seed is a pair of independent uniform exponents. The output is three group
elements. This expands the sampled space from cardinality \(q^2\) to
\(q^3\) [cite:@fioreCryptographicProtocolosApplicatons sec. 3.3.3].

* Security experiment and proof

The real experiment gives \(A\) the generated triple. The ideal experiment
gives three independent uniform group elements. Public parameters are supplied
in both. For independent uniform \(a,b,c\in\mathbb Z_q\),
\((g^a,g^b,g^c)\) is exactly uniform on \(\mathbb G^3\). Therefore

\[
\operatorname{PRGAdv}[A,G_{\mathrm{DH}}]
=\operatorname{DDHAdv}[A,\mathbb G].
\]

Under DDH, the generator is secure in the generalized finite-space definition.
This reduction has no advantage loss beyond the identical experiment.

* A statistical test exists

The image has exactly \(q^2\) elements: the first two coordinates uniquely
determine the exponents. An unbounded test can recover those exponents and
check the third coordinate. A uniform triple satisfies the relation with
probability \(1/q\), giving distinguishing advantage \(1-1/q\).

This does not contradict DDH, which restricts the test to polynomial time in
the security parameter.

* Representation caveat

The natural ideal output is uniform on \(\mathbb G^3\). Encoding the three
elements as fixed-width binary strings need not give a uniform distribution
over all strings of that length. An application requiring uniform-looking
binary pads needs an appropriate conversion and a proof for that distribution.
One must not substitute XOR encryption solely on the strength of the formula.
