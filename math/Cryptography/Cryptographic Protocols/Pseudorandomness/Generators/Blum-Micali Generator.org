Blum-Micali turns discrete-logarithm hardness into pseudorandom bits by
iterating a permutation and emitting a hard-core bit of each previous state.

* Permutation and predicate

Let \(p\) be an odd prime and \(g\) a generator of \(\mathbb Z_p^*\).
On \(S=\{1,\ldots,p-1\}\), define

\[
f(x)=g^x\bmod p,\qquad
h(x)=\begin{cases}
0,&1\leq x\leq(p-1)/2,\\
1,&(p-1)/2<x\leq p-1.
\end{cases}
\]

The map \(f\) is a permutation. Under the discrete logarithm assumption for
the chosen parameter family, the Blum-Micali hard-core theorem states that
\(h\) is hard-core for \(f\)
[cite:@fioreCryptographicProtocolosApplicatons secs. 2.6.1 and 3.3.1].

The predicate identifies the half of the exponent interval containing \(x\).
Calling it an MSB does not mean it is the leading bit of an arbitrary
fixed-width encoding.

* Generator

Sample uniform \(s_0\in S\). For polynomially many steps, compute

\[
r_i=h(s_{i-1}),\qquad s_i=g^{s_{i-1}}\bmod p,
\]

and output \(r_1\cdots r_t\). The modulus, generator, and parameter are
public; the initial state is secret.

* Security argument

The one-step pair \((h(S),f(S))\) is indistinguishable from a uniform bit and
an independent uniform state in \(S\). The hard-core theorem gives the first
replacement, and permutation uniformity gives the second component's exact
distribution. Sequential composition and projection away from the final state
then give an output indistinguishable from \(U_t\).

The ideal state is uniform on \(S\), not on all bitstrings of its encoding
length. The final output consists only of bits, so its ideal distribution is
indeed uniform on \(\{0,1\}^t\). The hard-core theorem is used here without
proof; it is not established by iteration alone.

* Small example

With \(p=11\), \(g=2\), and \(s_0=3\), the states alternate between
\(3\) and \(8\), emitting \(0101\ldots\). This tiny instance is visibly
insecure. It illustrates the recurrence and why the security statement must
refer to an appropriate growing parameter family.
