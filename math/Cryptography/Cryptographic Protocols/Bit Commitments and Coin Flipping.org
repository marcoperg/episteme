#+title: Bit Commitments and Coin Flipping
#+setupfile: ../../../setup.org

Bit commitment lets one party choose a bit while delaying its disclosure. The
lectures use it to prevent either participant in a telephone coin flip from
choosing their bit after learning the other participant's choice
[cite:@fioreCryptographicProtocolosApplicatons].

* Commitment requirements

A commitment has two phases:

1. In the *commit* phase, Alice sends a value that fixes a bit \(b_1\) without
   revealing it.
2. In the *open* phase, Alice reveals \(b_1\) and opening information that Bob
   verifies.

The security goals are:

- *Hiding:* before opening, Bob cannot determine the committed bit.
- *Binding:* after committing, Alice cannot successfully open the commitment as
  both \(0\) and \(1\).

These properties protect opposite parties and may rely on different kinds of
security.

* Coin flipping from commitment

Alice and Bob want a shared random bit \(b=b_1\oplus b_2\). If either party
announces a bit first, the other can choose the outcome. Commitment changes the
order:

1. Alice samples \(b_1\) and commits to it.
2. Bob samples and sends \(b_2\).
3. Alice opens the commitment.
4. Both output \(b_1\oplus b_2\).

Hiding prevents Bob from adapting \(b_2\) to \(b_1\); binding prevents Alice
from changing \(b_1\) after receiving \(b_2\).

* A PRG-based commitment

Let

\[
G:\{0,1\}^n\to\{0,1\}^{\ell},
\qquad \ell>3n.
\]

Bob samples \(r\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}\) and sends it to Alice. Alice samples
\(s\leftarrow\{0,1\}^n\) and commits to \(b\) with

\[
c=G(s)\oplus(b\cdot r),
\]

where \(0\cdot r=0^{\ell}\) and \(1\cdot r=r\). To open, she sends \((b,s)\),
and Bob verifies the same equation.

* Hiding intuition

For \(b=0\), the commitment is \(G(s)\); for \(b=1\), it is \(G(s)\oplus r\).
Pseudorandomness makes \(G(s)\) computationally indistinguishable from uniform.
Replacing it by a uniform string makes the two commitment distributions
identical, giving the ideal no-information argument. Thus hiding is
computational and reduces to PRG security.

* Statistical binding

Alice can open one commitment both ways only if there are seeds \(s_0,s_1\)
such that

\[
G(s_0)=G(s_1)\oplus r,
\]

or equivalently \(r=G(s_0)\oplus G(s_1)\). There are \(2^{2n}\) ordered seed
pairs, so there are at most \(2^{2n}\) bad values of \(r\). Therefore

\[
\Pr[r\text{ is bad}]
\leq\frac{2^{2n}}{2^{\ell}}
=2^{2n-\ell}
<2^{-n}.
\]

The count is an upper bound because different seed pairs can produce the same
bad value. Binding is therefore statistical over Bob's random choice of \(r\).

* Scope

This construction gives the hiding and binding ideas needed for the coin-flip
exchange. The lectures do not yet provide a complete malicious-party analysis,
including abort behavior or fairness guarantees.
