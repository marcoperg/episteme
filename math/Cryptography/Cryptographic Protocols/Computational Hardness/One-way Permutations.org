A one-way permutation combines computationally difficult inversion with an
exact distributional property: applying a bijection to a uniform input gives a
uniform output.

* Definition and inversion experiment

A one-way permutation is a one-way function family

\[
f_n:\{0,1\}^n\longrightarrow\{0,1\}^n
\]

in which every \(f_n\) is bijective. Sample uniform \(x\), give
\((1^n,f_n(x))\) to a PPT inverter, and accept exactly when its answer is
\(x\). This is the OWF experiment with a unique valid preimage. Its success
probability must be negligible
[cite:@fioreCryptographicProtocolosApplicatons sec. 2.4.1].

More generally, the same notion applies to a publicly described permutation of
an efficiently represented, uniformly sampleable finite set \(S\), with
negligibility measured in a stated security parameter.

* Uniformity is exact

If \(X\) is uniform on finite \(S\) and \(f:S\to S\) is bijective, then
for every \(y\in S\),

\[
\Pr[f(X)=y]=\Pr[X=f^{-1}(y)]=\frac1{|S|}.
\]

This fact is unconditional. One-wayness says that an efficient adversary
cannot usually compute that unique inverse, not that the inverse fails to
exist.

* Why permutations help construct PRGs

If \(h\) is hard-core for a bitstring permutation \(f\), then

\[
(f(U_n),h(U_n))\approx_c(f(U_n),U_1)=U_{n+1},
\]

where the independent random bit on the right is sampled separately. Thus
\(s\mapsto f(s)\mathbin\Vert h(s)\) is a one-bit expanding PRG.
For an arbitrary OWF, \(f(U_n)\) need not be uniform, so this proof fails.

* Examples and cautions

Under the appropriate assumptions, exponentiation on a carefully specified
finite set and squaring on quadratic residues modulo a Blum integer give
number-theoretic examples. Their binary encodings must not silently be treated
as uniform \(n\)-bit strings. Bijectivity alone is also insufficient: the
identity is a permutation but is easy to invert.

The existence of OWFs is not known to imply the existence of OWPs. The general
OWF-to-PRG theorem therefore goes beyond this elementary permutation route.
