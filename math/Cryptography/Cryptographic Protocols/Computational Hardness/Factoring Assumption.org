Multiplying large primes is efficient. The factoring assumption asserts that
recovering those factors from a randomly generated product is computationally
hard. It is a claim about a specified modulus distribution.

* Experiment and definition

Fix an efficient sampler for pairs of distinct \(n\)-bit primes \((p,q)\).
For example, sample uniformly from ordered distinct pairs of \(n\)-bit primes.
The factoring experiment computes \(N=pq\), gives \((1^n,N)\) to \(A\), and
accepts exactly when \(A\) returns admissible primes \(p',q'\) with

\[
p'q'=N.
\]

The *factoring assumption for this sampler* is that the acceptance probability
is negligible for every PPT \(A\). The probability includes prime generation
and adversarial coins. Prime sampling uses the efficient-sampling convention
of computational security
[cite:@fioreCryptographicProtocolosApplicatons sec. 2.4.2].

* A candidate one-way map

On this sampled domain, \(f(p,q)=pq\) is efficient to compute. An inverter
must return an admissible input pair; the trivial expression \(1\cdot N\)
is not a valid inverse. Either order of the prime factors is valid, so the
map is not a permutation when \(p\ne q\).

One may instead sample primes independently and allow \(p=q\), but then
that easy square-modulus event must be included in the analysis. Excluding
equal primes makes the convention explicit.

* Examples and distinctions

The product \(11\cdot13=143\) illustrates evaluation and inversion, but its
factors are easily found by trial division. Large size alone is not the whole
assumption: special modulus families may have different algorithms or attacks.

The distribution used by Blum-Blum-Shub additionally restricts both primes to
\(3\bmod4\). Hardness for that family must be stated for that sampler; it
does not follow merely by naming a different random-prime experiment.

Factoring also enables inversion of suitable RSA maps. The converse reduction
is not known in general, so factoring hardness must not be identified with
the RSA assumption.
