The DDH assumption says that the algebraic relation in a Diffie-Hellman triple
cannot be detected efficiently in the chosen group family.

* Definition and experiments

Public setup generates an efficiently represented cyclic group
\(\mathbb G=\langle g\rangle\) of prime order \(q\). Its description,
order, and generator are given to every algorithm. Sample independent uniform
\(a,b,c\in\mathbb Z_q\).

- *Real:* give \((g^a,g^b,g^{ab})\) to \(A\).
- *Random:* give \((g^a,g^b,g^c)\) to \(A\).

Define

\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{DDHAdv}[A,\mathbb G]=\big|&\Pr[A(g^a,g^b,g^{ab})=1]\\
&-\Pr[A(g^a,g^b,g^c)=1]\big|.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}

DDH holds for the parameter family if this is negligible for every PPT \(A\).
Probabilities also include public setup and adversarial coins
[cite:@fioreCryptographicProtocolosApplicatons sec. 3.3.3].

* What is being hidden?

Every component is a group element. In the random experiment, all three are
independent and uniform in \(\mathbb G\). The real triple is not actually
independent: its third exponent is the product of the first two. DDH says that
an efficient observer cannot exploit this difference.

A binary encoding of a uniform group element need not be uniform on all
bitstrings of that encoding length. The ideal distribution must match the
represented space, not a convenient but different bitstring space.

* Decisional versus computational assumptions

Computational Diffie-Hellman asks for \(g^{ab}\) given \(g^a,g^b\).
Discrete logarithm asks for an exponent such as \(a\). Either efficient
computation would enable a test for DDH triples, but the reverse implications
are not supplied by this definition. A group may permit efficient DDH testing
even when discrete logarithms are believed hard.
