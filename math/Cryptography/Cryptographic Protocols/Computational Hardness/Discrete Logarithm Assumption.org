Exponentiation in a suitable finite group is efficient; recovering a random
exponent from its group element is conjectured to be difficult. The claim
depends on the group family and its generation procedure.

* Problem and experiment

A public setup generates an efficiently represented cyclic group
\(\mathbb G=\langle g\rangle\) of known order \(q\). Group operations,
equality, and sampling are efficient. The inversion experiment is:

1. Generate and publish the group description, \(q\), and \(g\).
2. Sample uniform \(x\in\mathbb Z_q\) and compute \(y=g^x\).
3. Give \(y\) and the public parameters to \(A\).
4. Accept exactly when \(A\) returns \(x'\in\mathbb Z_q\) with \(g^{x'}=y\).

The *discrete logarithm assumption* says this success probability is negligible
for every PPT \(A\), over setup, the exponent, and adversarial coins
[cite:@fioreCryptographicProtocolosApplicatons sec. 2.4.2].

* Domains matter

The map \(x\mapsto g^x\) is a bijection
\(\mathbb Z_q\to\mathbb G\), but these are different represented sets.
It is not literally an \(n\)-bit permutation without additional conventions.

If \(p\) is prime and \(g\) generates \(\mathbb Z_p^*\), an alternative is

\[
f:\{1,\ldots,p-1\}\to\{1,\ldots,p-1\},\qquad f(x)=g^x\bmod p.
\]

Here the exponent \(p-1\) represents \(0\) modulo \(p-1\), making the map
a permutation of one finite set. Public setup must still select a group
family where inversion is assumed hard.

* Example and limitations

Modulo \(5\), the generator \(2\) gives
\(2^1=2\), \(2^2=4\), \(2^3=3\), and \(2^4=1\). This demonstrates the
bijection; inversion is trivial by enumeration at this size.

An exponentially large group is necessary but not sufficient for hardness:
the group structure and available algorithms matter. Likewise, hardness of
discrete logarithms does not by itself establish the DDH assumption, which is
a different, decisional claim.
