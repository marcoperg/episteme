The one-time pad hides a message by XORing it with an independent uniform key
of the same length. Its secrecy is unconditional, but its key cannot be reused.

* Construction and correctness

For \(L\geq1\), let
\(\mathcal K=\mathcal M=\mathcal C=\{0,1\}^L\), and define

\[
E(k,m)=k\oplus m,\qquad D(k,c)=k\oplus c.
\]

Correctness follows from
\(k\oplus(k\oplus m)=(k\oplus k)\oplus m=m\)
[cite:@fioreCryptographicProtocolosApplicatons secs. 1.2 and 1.3].

* Perfect secrecy

For fixed \(m,c\), exactly one key produces that ciphertext:
\(k=m\oplus c\). With a uniform key independent of the message,

\[
\Pr[E(K,m)=c]=2^{-L}.
\]

This distribution does not depend on \(m\). Thus observing a ciphertext does
not update any prior message distribution. An unbounded adversary has zero
distinguishing advantage, not zero probability of guessing the message.

The general definitions and Shannon's lower bound belong in
[[id:64853E70-8C7D-44AF-B1C4-DFE3B3FCFABC][Information-theoretic security]]. The bound explains why a perfectly secret
cipher cannot replace this long key by a shorter one.

* Worked example

Take \(m=1011\) and \(k=0110\). Then \(c=1101\), and
\(0110\oplus1101=1011\) recovers the message. These tiny strings illustrate
the operations, not a practical key size.

* Two different limitations

*Pad reuse* exposes a relation between messages:

\[
c_1\oplus c_2=(k\oplus m_1)\oplus(k\oplus m_2)=m_1\oplus m_2.
\]

Knowing either plaintext then reveals the other. Perfect secrecy for one
message does not imply secrecy for repeated use of that same key.

*Malleability* is a separate issue. Replacing \(c\) by \(c\oplus\Delta\)
makes Bob recover \(m\oplus\Delta\), without an intrinsic indication of
tampering. Even a correctly used one-time pad does not authenticate a message.
