RSA needs three distinct facts: arithmetic modulo a public integer is
efficient; units admit exponent reduction; and a secret factorization makes an
inverse exponent computable. These statements have different hypotheses.

* Residues, units, and division

Represent \(\mathbb Z_N=\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z\), for \(N\geq2\), by
\(0,\ldots,N-1\). It is a commutative ring. Its multiplicative group is

\[
\mathbb Z_N^*=\{x\in\mathbb Z_N:\gcd(x,N)=1\}.
\]

An element \(x\) has a multiplicative inverse exactly when
\(\gcd(x,N)=1\): the extended Euclidean algorithm then finds \(u,v\) with
\(ux+vN=1\), so \(u\bmod N=x^{-1}\). Conversely, an inverse gives such
an integer identity, forcing the gcd to be \(1\).

Division by a unit means \(y/x=yx^{-1}\pmod N\), not real division followed
by a remainder. For example, \(3^{-1}=7\pmod{10}\), so
\(8/3=6\pmod{10}\). The nonunit \(4\) has no inverse modulo \(10\);
\(4u=8\pmod{10}\) has two solutions, while \(4u=7\pmod{10}\) has none
[cite:@fioreCryptographicProtocolosApplicatons secs. 8.4 and 8.6.1].

Gcd computation tests unit membership without factoring \(N\). Rejection
sampling uniform residues gives uniform units. For \(N=pq\) with large
distinct primes, the acceptance probability is
\((1-1/p)(1-1/q)\), close to \(1\).

* Totients and the Chinese remainder theorem

For coprime \(a,b\), reduction modulo each factor gives the ring isomorphism

\[
\mathbb Z_{ab}\cong\mathbb Z_a\times\mathbb Z_b.
\]

Equal reductions imply a difference divisible by \(a\) and \(b\), hence
by \(ab\); injectivity and equal finite cardinalities prove bijectivity.
Restricting to units gives
\(\mathbb Z_{ab}^*\cong\mathbb Z_a^*\times\mathbb Z_b^*\).

The totient is \(\varphi(N)=|\mathbb Z_N^*|\). If
\(N=\prod_i p_i^{e_i}\) is its prime factorization, then

\[
\varphi(N)=\prod_i p_i^{e_i-1}(p_i-1)
=N\prod_i\left(1-\frac1{p_i}\right).
\]

Indeed, exactly \(p^{e-1}\) residues modulo \(p^e\) are divisible by \(p\),
and CRT multiplies the unit counts for coprime factors. In particular,
\(\varphi(pq)=(p-1)(q-1)\) for /distinct/ primes; for a square,
\(\varphi(p^2)=p(p-1)\) instead
[cite:@fioreCryptographicProtocolosApplicatons sec. 8.4.1].

* Euler's theorem and exponent reduction

For \(x\in\mathbb Z_N^*\), multiplication by \(x\) permutes all units.
Equating their products and cancelling the common unit product gives

\[
x^{\varphi(N)}=1\pmod N.
\]

Thus \(x^a=x^{a\bmod\varphi(N)}\pmod N\), with negative powers defined
using inverses. For a prime \(p\), this gives Fermat's identity
\(x^{p-1}=1\pmod p\) for nonzero residues, and \(x^p=x\pmod p\) for
all residues after treating zero separately.

Euler's identity requires a unit: \(2^{\varphi(6)}=4\ne1\pmod6\).
Reducing exponents also requires knowing the relevant group order
[cite:@fioreCryptographicProtocolosApplicatons secs. 8.5 and 8.6.2].

* Efficient exponentiation without a factorization

To compute \(x^a\bmod N\) for \(a\geq0\), initialize
\(r=1,z=x\bmod N,e=a\). Repeatedly:

1. If \(e\) is odd, set \(r\leftarrow rz\bmod N\).
2. Set \(z\leftarrow z^2\bmod N\) and \(e\leftarrow\lfloor e/2\rfloor\).

The invariant \(rz^e=x^a\pmod N\) proves correctness when \(e=0\).
There are \(O(\log(a+1))\) modular multiplications, always on
\(O(\log N)\)-bit residues. This is polynomial-time arithmetic, without
constructing the enormous integer \(x^a\).

Repeated squaring works for nonunits too and requires neither a factorization
nor \(\varphi(N)\). It must not be confused with exponent reduction.
