Naor's commitment uses a long pseudorandom string to hide a bit and a receiver's
random challenge to make double openings statistically unlikely.

* Protocol

Let \(G:\{0,1\}^n\to\{0,1\}^L\) be a secure PRG, with polynomially
bounded \(L\geq3n\). Alice commits to \(b\in\{0,1\}\):

1. Bob samples uniform \(r\in\{0,1\}^L\) and sends it to Alice.
2. Alice rejects a malformed challenge. Otherwise she samples a fresh uniform
   \(s\in\{0,1\}^n\) and sends \(c=G(s)\oplus(b\cdot r)\).
3. To open, Alice sends \((b,s)\). Bob checks their types and accepts exactly
   when \(c=G(s)\oplus(b\cdot r)\).

Here \(0\cdot r=0^L\) and \(1\cdot r=r\). Correctness follows by
recomputing the commitment equation
[cite:@fioreCryptographicProtocolosApplicatons sec. 3.4.3].

* Computational hiding

Replace \(G(s)\) with an independent uniform \(U\in\{0,1\}^L\).
Conditional on \(r\) and any prior receiver state, \(U\oplus(b\cdot r)\)
is uniform for either bit. Thus the two ideal views coincide.

For each fixed bit, distinguishing its real and ideal views gives a PRG test:
simulate the receiver to obtain \(r\), use the PRG challenge as the pad, and
continue its computation. A triangle inequality over these two replacements
proves hiding. This works even for a malicious receiver choosing \(r\),
because its challenge precedes the committer's fresh seed.

* Statistical binding

A commitment that opens both ways has seeds \(s_0,s_1\) satisfying

\[
c=G(s_0)=G(s_1)\oplus r,
\qquad r=G(s_0)\oplus G(s_1).
\]

There are at most \(2^{2n}\) such challenges, one per ordered seed pair.
With an honest uniform \(r\),

\[
\Pr[\text{some commitment admits both openings}]
\leq 2^{2n-L}\leq2^{-n}.
\]

Outside this bad set, no computation can find a double opening because none
exists. Binding is therefore statistical, and does not itself use PRG
hardness. The count is an upper bound: different seed pairs can give the same
challenge.

* Different adversarial roles

Binding requires an honest receiver's random challenge. For \(r=0^L\),
both bit formulas are identical, so binding fails. This does not contradict
hiding against a malicious receiver; the two properties protect different
participants under different honesty assumptions.
