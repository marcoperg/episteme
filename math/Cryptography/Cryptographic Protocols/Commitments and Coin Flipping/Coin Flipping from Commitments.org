Two remote parties can combine private bits into a common coin, but a simple
exchange lets the last sender choose the outcome. A commitment fixes one bit
before the other is revealed. It does not force the protocol to finish.

* Why a direct exchange fails

If independent uniform bits \(b_1,b_2\) are combined as
\(b=b_1\oplus b_2\), the result is uniform. Independence is the difficulty.
After seeing \(b_1\), Bob can force target \(v\) by setting
\(b_2=b_1\oplus v\). Reversing the order just changes who can cheat.

* Protocol

Use Naor's computationally hiding, statistically binding commitment:

1. Bob sends the random commitment challenge \(r\).
2. Alice samples uniform \(b_1\), commits to it using a fresh seed, and sends
   the commitment \(c\).
3. Bob samples an independent uniform \(b_2\) and sends it to Alice.
4. Alice opens \(c\) by sending \((b_1,s)\).
5. Bob verifies. On acceptance, the agreed output is \(b_1\oplus b_2\);
   on an invalid or missing opening, Bob outputs \(\bot\).

Honest execution gives the same exactly uniform bit to both parties
[cite:@fioreCryptographicProtocolosApplicatons sec. 3.4.4].

* Security objective and reasoning

For each fixed target \(v\in\{0,1\}\), a PPT cheating participant can make
the honest participant output \(v\) with probability at most
\(1/2+\operatorname{negl}(n)\). An abort is a separate outcome \(\bot\),
not a successful target bit.

Against cheating Alice, statistical binding fixes any acceptable \(b_1\)
before Bob sends his independent \(b_2\), except on a negligible set of
challenges. Alice may decline to open, but cannot usually change her bit.

Against cheating Bob, computational hiding prevents his choice of \(b_2\)
from usefully correlating with Alice's hidden uniform \(b_1\). An
improvement in forcing a chosen XOR value would predict \(b_1\) and violate
hiding. Bob's earlier choice of challenge is included in the malicious
receiver's view.

* Selective abort: an important limitation

Alice learns the prospective result when \(b_2\) arrives. She can open only
when \(b_1\oplus b_2=0\). Then every completed execution produces \(0\),
while the other executions abort.

This does not exceed probability \(1/2\) of making Bob output \(0\), but
it makes the result completely biased *conditioned on completion*. The
protocol provides a coin flip with abort, not guaranteed delivery of a fair
coin. Hiding and binding alone do not supply that stronger fairness property.
