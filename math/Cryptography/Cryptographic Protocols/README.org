* About these notes

Concept-oriented notes from the Cryptographic Protocols and Applications
course in the Formal Methods for Computer Science master's program, 2026/27.
Each note explains one main notion, construction, or proof technique, with
precise definitions, experiments where relevant, examples, and explicit limits.

The course follows Boneh and Shoup's /A Graduate Course in Applied
Cryptography/, available at [[https://toc.cryptobook.us][toc.cryptobook.us]]
[cite:@bonehGraduateCourseApplied]. Claim-level references point to the
course manuscript [cite:@fioreCryptographicProtocolosApplicatons].

* Scope and conventions

These 57 concept notes cover chapters 1--9 of the course manuscript: computational
security, one-way assumptions, PRGs, commitments, PRFs and PRPs, secret-key
encryption and modes, MACs, trapdoors and RSA, and public-key hybrid encryption.
Perfect secrecy is reused from [[id:64853E70-8C7D-44AF-B1C4-DFE3B3FCFABC][Information-theoretic security]], rather than
duplicated. The RSA arithmetic note links to the existing algebraic context.

Section locators refer to the manuscript currently attached to the course's
Better BibTeX entry. Bibliographic titles and citekeys are retained as catalogued,
including existing spelling errors. Definitions and concrete bounds keep the
manuscript's distinction between distinguishing and bit-guessing advantages.

Digital signatures, authenticated key exchange, authenticated encryption, and
chosen-ciphertext security remain outside the established constructions here.
They are not consequences of the CPA, MAC, or passive-recovery theorems.

The security parameter is \(\lambda\), or \(n\) when seed length indexes the
family. Uniform samples are independent unless a dependence is stated.
\(U_t\) denotes a uniform \(t\)-bit string; \(\Vert\) is concatenation and
\(\oplus\) is bitwise XOR. Public setup is available to the adversary.
Computational adversaries are classical and uniform. Every security claim is
relative to its stated experiment; confidentiality does not imply authenticity.

* Suggested reading route

1. Encryption schemes, one-time pad, and perfect secrecy.
2. Computational security, negligible functions, indistinguishability, and
   one-time semantic security.
3. One-way functions and permutations, hard-core predicates, Goldreich-Levin,
   and pseudorandom generators.
4. Stretch amplification, reductions, and hybrid arguments; then encryption,
   stream ciphers, and commitments as applications.
5. Random functions, lazy sampling, PRFs, GGM, and Naor-Reingold.
6. Secret-key chosen-plaintext security and randomized PRF-based encryption;
   then PRPs, switching, CTR, ECB's failure, CBC, and Luby-Rackoff.
7. MAC syntax, fresh-message versus strong unforgeability, and PRF-based tags.
8. Anonymous key exchange and trapdoors; modular arithmetic, RSA correctness
   and its inversion assumption, then fixed-domain RSA and raw key establishment.
9. Public-key encryption and the one-to-many challenge reduction; the
   random-oracle model, hybrid encryption, its hidden-preimage proof, and RSA
   instantiated with a one-time pad.
