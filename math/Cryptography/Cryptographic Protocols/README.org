* About these notes

Concept-oriented notes from the Cryptographic Protocols and Applications
course in the Formal Methods for Computer Science master's program, 2026/27.
Each note explains one main notion, construction, or proof technique, with
precise definitions, experiments where relevant, examples, and explicit limits.

The course follows Boneh and Shoup's /A Graduate Course in Applied
Cryptography/, available at [[https://toc.cryptobook.us][toc.cryptobook.us]]
[cite:@bonehGraduateCourseApplied]. Claim-level references point to the
course manuscript [cite:@fioreCryptographicProtocolosApplicatons].

* Scope and conventions

These 35 notes cover computational security, one-way assumptions, PRGs,
commitments, PRFs, and the basic PRF-based CPA-secure cipher. Perfect secrecy
is reused from [[id:64853E70-8C7D-44AF-B1C4-DFE3B3FCFABC][Information-theoretic security]], rather than duplicated.

The current BibTeX attachment is an 86-page manuscript. Its chapters 1--4 and
sections 5.1--5.3 are the main source for this selection. Later material on
modes, authentication, RSA, and public-key encryption is outside this pass.
Bibliotheca's Org catalogue still lists an older 41-page attachment; section
locators here refer to the newer manuscript. Bibliographic titles and citekeys
are retained as catalogued, including existing spelling errors in source titles.

The security parameter is \(\lambda\), or \(n\) when seed length indexes the
family. Uniform samples are independent unless a dependence is stated.
\(U_t\) denotes a uniform \(t\)-bit string; \(\Vert\) is concatenation and
\(\oplus\) is bitwise XOR. Public setup is available to the adversary.
Computational adversaries are classical and uniform. Every security claim is
relative to its stated experiment; confidentiality does not imply authenticity.

* Suggested reading route

1. Encryption schemes, one-time pad, and perfect secrecy.
2. Computational security, negligible functions, indistinguishability, and
   one-time semantic security.
3. One-way functions and permutations, hard-core predicates, Goldreich-Levin,
   and pseudorandom generators.
4. Stretch amplification, reductions, and hybrid arguments; then encryption,
   stream ciphers, and commitments as applications.
5. Random functions, lazy sampling, PRFs, GGM, and Naor-Reingold.
6. Chosen-plaintext security and randomized PRF-based encryption, including
   their collision bounds.
