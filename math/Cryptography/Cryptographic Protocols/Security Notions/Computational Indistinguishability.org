Two distributions are computationally indistinguishable when no efficient test
can tell which one supplied its sample with non-negligible advantage. They need
not be equal, or even statistically close.

* Definition

Let \(X=(X_\lambda)_\lambda\) and \(Y=(Y_\lambda)_\lambda\) be families of
distributions on efficiently represented strings of polynomially bounded
length. For a PPT algorithm \(A\), define

\[
\Delta_A(X,Y;\lambda)=
\left|\Pr[A(1^\lambda,X_\lambda)=1]
-\Pr[A(1^\lambda,Y_\lambda)=1]\right|.
\]

We write \(X\approx_c Y\) if this quantity is negligible for every PPT
\(A\). Probabilities include the sampled input and the coins of \(A\).
This is the common distributional form underlying the course's encryption,
PRG, and hard-core experiments
[cite:@fioreCryptographicProtocolosApplicatons secs. 2.2, 2.3.1 and 2.6].

* Distinguishing experiment

For a fixed bit \(b\), the challenger samples from \(X_\lambda\) when
\(b=0\) and from \(Y_\lambda\) when \(b=1\), and gives the sample to \(A\).
The advantage is the absolute difference between the probabilities that \(A\)
outputs \(1\). A single-sample definition does not automatically give an
oracle-access guarantee.

* Examples and limits

Equal distributions are computationally indistinguishable, with zero advantage.
A secure PRG gives a more interesting example:
\(G(U_n)\approx_c U_{L(n)}\), although its output has at most \(2^n\)
possible values. An unbounded test can enumerate those values.

The adversary's information is part of the definition. Indistinguishability of
\(X\) and \(Y\) does not justify attaching arbitrary correlated side
information and assuming that \((X,Z)\approx_c(Y,Z)\).

* Useful properties

- *Symmetry:* \(X\approx_c Y\) implies \(Y\approx_c X\).
- *Transitivity:* if \(X\approx_c Y\) and \(Y\approx_c Z\), then
  \(X\approx_c Z\), by the triangle inequality.
- *Efficient processing:* if \(T\) is an efficient randomized transformation,
  using fresh independent coins, then \(T(X)\approx_c T(Y)\). A distinguisher
  for the outputs could be composed with \(T\) to distinguish the inputs.

Polynomially long chains need a uniform reduction and a controlled loss, not
merely a separate asymptotic statement about every fixed adjacent pair.
