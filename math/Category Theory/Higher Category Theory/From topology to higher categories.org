* The guiding question

Why do we pass from ordinary categories to higher categories? A useful route is
to begin with topology and ask what happens when we stop identifying things
merely because they can be continuously deformed into one another.

The progression has two distinct ingredients:

1. *Keep the witnesses of equivalence, and the witnesses between witnesses.*
   This leads from a space to an \(\infty\)-groupoid.
2. *Allow some of the arrows to be genuinely directed.* This leads from
   \((\infty,0)\)-categories to \((\infty,1)\)-categories, then to
   \((\infty,n)\)-categories.

The notation \((\infty,n)\) means that morphisms may occur in arbitrarily high
dimensions, but *every morphism of dimension greater than \(n\) is invertible*,
in the appropriate higher-categorical sense. It does not mean that every
morphism of dimension at most \(n\) must be noninvertible.

* Start with Top: spaces and continuous maps

The ordinary [[id:6B755D9E-EB8E-4673-ADD0-B4411D410D52][category]] \(\mathrm{Top}\) has topological spaces as objects and
continuous maps as morphisms. Composition is ordinary composition of functions.
Its isomorphisms are homeomorphisms.

Homotopy theory asks for a coarser notion of sameness. For example, the interval
\([0,1]\) and a one-point space are not homeomorphic, but the interval contracts
to a point. They have the same homotopy type.

Two maps \(f,g:X\to Y\) are *homotopic* if there is a continuous map
\[
H:X\times[0,1]\longrightarrow Y,
\qquad H(x,0)=f(x),\quad H(x,1)=g(x).
\]
The map \(H\) is a deformation from \(f\) to \(g\). A homotopy equivalence is a
map \(f:X\to Y\) with a map \(g:Y\to X\) and homotopies
\[
g\circ f\simeq\mathrm{id}_X,
\qquad f\circ g\simeq\mathrm{id}_Y.
\]
Unlike a homeomorphism, it has an inverse only /up to homotopy/.

We can form an ordinary category with homotopy classes of maps. But doing so
forgets which homotopies relate the maps, whether two homotopies are themselves
homotopic, and so on. This is the first reason to introduce higher morphisms:
*a homotopy is mathematical data, not merely permission to declare equality*.

For general spaces, modern homotopy theory usually works with *weak homotopy
equivalences*: maps inducing bijections on path components and isomorphisms on
all based homotopy groups. On CW complexes, weak homotopy equivalences are
homotopy equivalences by Whitehead's theorem. We will distinguish the homotopy
type of one space from the category that organizes all such types.

* One space gives an infinity-groupoid: (infinity,0)

** Points, paths, and higher homotopies

Fix a space \(X\). Its topology suggests the following hierarchy:

#+ATTR_LATEX: :align >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{\dimexpr0.18\linewidth-2\tabcolsep\relax} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{\dimexpr0.30\linewidth-2\tabcolsep\relax} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{\dimexpr0.52\linewidth-2\tabcolsep\relax}
| Dimension | Categorical interpretation | Topological data |
|-----------+----------------------------+------------------|
|         0 | Objects                    | Points of \(X\) |
|         1 | Morphisms                  | Paths between points |
|         2 | Morphisms between morphisms | Homotopies between paths, fixing endpoints |
|         3 | Morphisms between 2-morphisms | Homotopies between those homotopies, fixing boundary data |
|       ... | ...                        | ... |

There is no universal dimension at which this process stops. Higher homotopy
groups can carry information arbitrarily far up the hierarchy.

Why is this a /groupoid/? A path \(\gamma:x\to y\) can be reversed to give
\(\bar\gamma:y\to x\). The concatenation \(\bar\gamma\circ\gamma\) is generally
not literally the constant path, but it is homotopic to it. Thus paths are
invertible *up to 2-morphisms*. Reversing a homotopy similarly produces an
inverse up to a higher homotopy. The same pattern continues in every positive
dimension.

This is an *\(\infty\)-groupoid*, also called an *\((\infty,0)\)-category*:
objects need not be invertible, but all positive-dimensional morphisms are.

** Why a fundamental groupoid is not enough

The fundamental groupoid \(\Pi_1(X)\) has points as objects and
endpoint-preserving homotopy classes of paths as arrows. Passing to classes
makes path inverses and associativity exact, but discards the higher layers.

For example, \(S^2\) is connected and simply connected, so \(\Pi_1(S^2)\) is
equivalent to the one-object trivial groupoid. The same is true of a point.
Nevertheless,
\[
\pi_2(S^2)\cong\mathbb{Z},
\qquad \pi_2(*)=0.
\]
A fundamental groupoid cannot distinguish these homotopy types. The
\(\infty\)-groupoid keeps the information that distinguishes them.

At a chosen point \(x\), \(\pi_1(X,x)\) consists of loops modulo homotopy;
\(\pi_2(X,x)\) can be understood using 2-morphisms from the identity path to
itself, modulo 3-morphisms; higher homotopy groups continue this pattern.

** Weak composition and coherence

With ordinary parametrized paths, concatenation is associative only up to
reparametrization. The two paths
\[
(\gamma_3\circ\gamma_2)\circ\gamma_1,
\qquad
\gamma_3\circ(\gamma_2\circ\gamma_1)
\]
are related by a specified homotopy rather than necessarily being equal.
Different ways of using such homotopies must themselves agree up to higher
homotopies. These are *coherence data*.

Some models make selected operations strictly associative, for example by
using Moore paths. The essential point is not that strict formulas are
impossible, but that the full homotopy information must survive. A weak higher
category systematically records composition and its higher coherences.

** The homotopy hypothesis

The *homotopy hypothesis* says that \(\infty\)-groupoids model homotopy types:
the homotopy theory of spaces and the homotopy theory of
\(\infty\)-groupoids are equivalent. Here “space” means its weak homotopy type,
not all its point-set topological features.

A concrete model is the singular simplicial set
\[
\operatorname{Sing}(X)_k
=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathrm{Top}}(\Delta^k,X).
\]
Its simplices encode points, paths, triangles, and higher-dimensional data.
It is a *Kan complex*, a standard model of an \(\infty\)-groupoid. Geometric
realization recovers the weak homotopy type. These simplices are a precise
encoding of the hierarchy above, rather than a literal list of globular
homotopies.

Thus topology motivates \((\infty,0)\)-categories because a homotopy type
contains reversible comparisons in every dimension. Ordinary categories can
still be used to study it, but a category of homotopy classes forgets part of
the structure.

* Many spaces and their maps give an (infinity,1)-category

** The decisive change: maps need not be reversible

There are now two different levels to keep apart:

- *Inside one space \(X\):* objects are points, and paths are invertible up to
  homotopy. This gives an \((\infty,0)\)-category.
- *Between spaces:* objects are homotopy types, and arrows are maps between
  them. These maps need not be equivalences.

For example, the collapse map \(S^1\to *\) has no homotopy inverse: it destroys
the nontrivial fundamental group. Homotopy theory needs this map just as much
as it needs equivalences. We therefore cannot organize all spaces and maps as
an \(\infty\)-groupoid.

Instead, organize them as follows:

#+ATTR_LATEX: :align >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{\dimexpr0.18\linewidth-2\tabcolsep\relax} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{\dimexpr0.82\linewidth-2\tabcolsep\relax}
| Dimension | Data |
|-----------+------|
|         0 | Spaces, understood up to weak homotopy equivalence |
|         1 | Maps between spaces; not necessarily invertible |
|         2 | Homotopies between maps |
|         3 | Homotopies between homotopies |
|       ... | Higher homotopies |

Every layer above dimension 1 is reversible up to further homotopy, but the
maps in dimension 1 may be genuinely directed. This is the
*\((\infty,1)\)-category of spaces*, usually denoted \(\mathcal{S}\).

More precisely, \(\mathcal{S}\) is obtained by /higher-categorical localization/
of \(\mathrm{Top}\) at weak homotopy equivalences. Localization makes these maps
equivalences while retaining higher information about maps. It does not make
every map invertible. Using suitably convenient spaces and replacements gives
concrete models of this construction.

** Hom-sets become mapping spaces

In an ordinary category, \(\operatorname{Hom}(X,Y)\) is a set. In
\(\mathcal{S}\), it is replaced by a *mapping space*
\(\operatorname{Map}_{\mathcal{S}}(X,Y)\):

- its points represent maps \(X\to Y\);
- its paths represent homotopies between maps;
- its higher structure represents higher homotopies.

This is a derived mapping space: raw continuous function spaces may require
replacements to compute it correctly. For CW-type sources and suitable target
models, the familiar mapping-space picture realizes this intuition.

Taking path components recovers the hom-set in the ordinary homotopy category:
\[
\operatorname{Hom}_{h\mathcal{S}}(X,Y)
=\pi_0\operatorname{Map}_{\mathcal{S}}(X,Y).
\]
Thus an \((\infty,1)\)-category has an ordinary homotopy category, but retains
what taking \(\pi_0\) forgets.

For a particularly simple example,
\[
\operatorname{Map}_{\mathcal{S}}(*,Y)\simeq Y.
\]
Consequently, maps from a point to \(S^2\) have a mapping space equivalent to
\(S^2\), although their set of homotopy classes has just one element. Losing
the mapping space loses its \(\pi_2\), among other data.

** Why the extra data matters in constructions

A homotopy pullback of \(X\xrightarrow{f}Z\xleftarrow{g}Y\) can be modeled by
triples
\[
(x,y,\gamma),\qquad \gamma:f(x)\leadsto g(y).
\]
The path \(\gamma\) is a /chosen witness/ of agreement, not simply a yes-or-no
condition that the images lie in the same path component. Its own homotopies
contribute to the homotopy type of the pullback.

For example, the homotopy pullback of
\(*\xrightarrow{z}Z\xleftarrow{z}*\) is the based loop space \(\Omega_z Z\).
The ordinary pullback is just a point. Retaining coherent witnesses produces
a genuinely different construction.

This is why \((\infty,1)\)-categories are useful for homotopy theory and derived
mathematics: they keep noninvertible maps while allowing universal properties
and diagrams to remember their full homotopy-coherent content.

* Why go beyond (infinity,1)? Directed comparisons between maps

An \((\infty,1)\)-category is appropriate when every comparison /between
arrows/ is reversible. But many mathematical comparisons are not.

Consider categories, functors, and [[id:F23D46CF-CE5C-4996-A3D3-FCC83C03F9A8][natural transformations]]:

- objects: categories \(C,D,\ldots\);
- 1-morphisms: functors \(F:C\to D\);
- 2-morphisms: natural transformations \(\eta:F\Rightarrow G\).

A natural transformation need not be a natural isomorphism. For instance, let
\(\mathbf{1}\) be the terminal category and let \([1]\) be the category
\(0\to1\). The functors \(F,G:\mathbf{1}\to[1]\) selecting \(0\) and \(1\)
have a natural transformation \(F\Rightarrow G\), but none in the reverse
direction. This is a genuinely noninvertible 2-morphism.

The resulting strict 2-category \(\mathrm{Cat}\) can be viewed as an
\((\infty,2)\)-category with only identity higher cells. To include its arbitrary
natural transformations as 2-morphisms, an \((\infty,1)\)-category is insufficient.

There is nevertheless an important \((\infty,1)\)-category
\(\mathrm{Cat}_\infty\) of \((\infty,1)\)-categories. Its mapping spaces retain
functors and /natural equivalences/. To retain all natural transformations as
2-morphisms, one uses an \((\infty,2)\)-category instead. The choice depends on
which comparisons the problem needs to remember.

Another example is the bicategory of rings and bimodules: rings are objects,
bimodules are 1-morphisms, and bimodule homomorphisms are 2-morphisms. Composition
uses tensor products and is associative up to coherent isomorphism. Bimodule
homomorphisms need not be invertible. Derived versions of this example also
retain nontrivial higher homotopies.

* The general pattern: (infinity,n)

An *\((\infty,n)\)-category* allows genuinely noninvertible morphisms through
dimension \(n\), while every morphism above dimension \(n\) is invertible.
The inverse laws are understood up to coherent higher equivalence, not
necessarily as strict equations.

The need to increase \(n\) arises when the next layer of comparisons must be
allowed to be directed:

- \(n=0\): all comparisons are reversible; homotopy types.
- \(n=1\): maps may be directed; homotopies between them are reversible.
- \(n=2\): comparisons between maps may also be directed; for example,
  arbitrary natural transformations.
- \(n=3\): comparisons between 2-morphisms may also be directed; for example,
  modifications between suitable transformations of 2-functors.

In the last example, a modification has components that are 2-morphisms in the
target 2-category. Those components need not be invertible. Thus the familiar
pattern of categories, functors, and transformations can be iterated, producing
a need for a third directed layer and then further ones.

A useful recursive intuition, for \(n\geq1\), is
\[
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(x,y)
\quad\text{is an }(\infty,n-1)\text{-category}.
\]
Its objects are 1-morphisms of \(\mathcal{C}\), its 1-morphisms are
2-morphisms of \(\mathcal{C}\), and so on. In particular:

- an \((\infty,1)\)-category has mapping /spaces/;
- an \((\infty,2)\)-category has mapping /\((\infty,1)\)-categories/;
- an \((\infty,3)\)-category has mapping /\((\infty,2)\)-categories/.

This is a guide to the structure, not a complete definition: composition,
units, and coherence must also be supplied in a chosen model.

The two parameters answer different questions. The first, \(\infty\), says
there is no fixed bound on the dimensions of coherence data. The second,
\(n\), gives the bound above which /invertibility/ is required. Increasing
\(n\) changes which cells may be directed; it does not merely add more homotopy
groups to a space. Even a space with nontrivial homotopy groups in arbitrarily
high degrees is still an \((\infty,0)\)-category.

An ordinary category embeds as an \((\infty,1)\)-category with discrete mapping
spaces. Similarly, an ordinary groupoid gives an \(\infty\)-groupoid, but models
only a homotopy 1-type. Higher categories extend these examples rather than
requiring every higher layer to be nontrivial.

* And (infinity,infinity)? No invertibility cutoff

The same idea suggests an *\((\infty,\infty)\)-category*: morphisms in arbitrarily
high dimensions, with *no dimension above which invertibility is required*.
This is often discussed in terms of weak \(\omega\)-categories; precise
definitions and conventions depend on the model.

Here one can have directed processes, directed comparisons between processes,
directed comparisons between those comparisons, and so on without a fixed
cutoff. Some or even all cells may still be invertible; the definition simply
does not force eventual invertibility.

One elementary source of intuition is to iterate “category of higher
categories, functors, transformations, modifications, ...”. At any fixed stage
one obtains only finitely many potentially directed layers. Allowing the
hierarchy itself to continue indefinitely motivates the unbounded notion.
Constructing it coherently requires a genuine model of higher categories, not
just an informal union of the finite stages.

For ordinary homotopy theory of spaces, no such extension is necessary:
homotopies remain reversible. The reason to go to \((\infty,\infty)\) is an
unbounded hierarchy of /directed/ comparisons, not a failure of
\(\infty\)-groupoids to capture higher homotopy groups.

* Summary: two moves, not one ladder of spaces

- *One space:* retain the full homotopy type of \(X\in\mathrm{Top}\) as
  \(\Pi_\infty(X)\), an \((\infty,0)\)-category.
- *Spaces together with all maps:* organize them in \(\mathcal{S}\), an
  \((\infty,1)\)-category.
- *Directed comparisons through dimension \(n\):* use
  \((\infty,n)\)-categories.
- *Directed comparisons with no dimension bound:* use
  \((\infty,\infty)\)-categories.

The first move retains equivalence witnesses in all dimensions. The second
allows directed morphisms in successively higher dimensions. Together they
explain why higher category theory contains both homotopy theory and the
categorical mathematics of functors, transformations, and their higher analogues.

* Further reading

The following is a reading guide, not a claim that every source was used to
write this note. The first group is already recorded in Bibliotheca, with PDF
attachment links in its metadata; this does not guarantee that every attachment
is currently downloaded. The second group consists of external suggestions not
found in the current bibliography.

** Already in Bibliotheca

*** Topology and the (infinity,0) viewpoint

- *Allen Hatcher, /Algebraic Topology/*
  [cite:@allenhatcherAlgebraicTopology2001].
  Background for the opening of this note: homotopy equivalences, the
  fundamental group, CW complexes, and higher homotopy groups. Start with
  Chapter 0 and the fundamental-group material in Chapter 1; return to
  Chapter 4 for higher homotopy theory. This is a topology textbook, not an
  introduction to models of higher categories.
- *Paul G. Goerss and John F. Jardine, /Simplicial Homotopy Theory/*
  [cite:@goerssSimplicialHomotopyTheory].
  The technical bridge from spaces to simplicial sets, Kan complexes, and
  their homotopy theory. Use it when the singular-complex discussion above
  raises questions about how the combinatorial model actually works. More
  demanding than Hatcher; best treated as a foundational reference.
- */Homotopy Type Theory: Univalent Foundations of Mathematics/*
  [cite:@HomotopyTypeTheory].
  An optional alternative route: identity types retain paths and iterated
  identity types retain higher paths. Chapters 2 and 6 are especially relevant
  to proof-relevant equality and higher inductive types. This illuminates the
  reversible hierarchy, but is not a replacement for a theory of general
  directed \((\infty,n)\)-categories.

*** Making (infinity,1)-categories precise

- *Vladimir Hinich, /Lectures on Infinity Categories/*
  [cite:@hinichLecturesInfinityCategories2018].
  A lecture-note route into the technical theory. A good next step after an
  informal survey, particularly if you want to work with concrete models and
  localization rather than remain at the level of the path analogy.
- *Emily Riehl and Dominic Verity, /Elements of \(\infty\)-Category Theory/*
  [cite:@riehlElementsCategoryTheory2022].
  Develops \((\infty,1)\)-category theory using the model-independent framework
  of an \(\infty\)-cosmos. Especially useful for understanding how adjunctions,
  limits, and other familiar categorical constructions survive in the higher
  setting. Requires a solid command of ordinary category theory; its use of
  2-categorical methods does not mean its objects are general
  \((\infty,2)\)-categories.
- *Jacob Lurie, /Higher Topos Theory/*
  [cite:@lurieHigherToposTheory2009].
  A foundational reference for the quasi-category approach to
  \((\infty,1)\)-categories and higher topoi. Read the introductory motivation
  first, then consult it for precise constructions and results. It is not the
  gentlest first textbook, nor a general treatment of every
  \((\infty,n)\)-category.

*** Directed higher morphisms and coherence

- *Dimitri Ara, Albert Burroni, Yves Guiraud, Philippe Malbos, François
  Métayer, and Samuel Mimram, /Polygraphs: From Rewriting to Higher Categories/*
  [cite:@araPolygraphsRewritingHigher2025].
  A complementary route through rewriting, presentations, and coherence in
  strict higher categories. Begin with the low-dimensional material to see
  concrete directed higher cells. Keep the distinction between strict and weak
  higher categories in mind: this is not simply another presentation of all
  the homotopy-coherent structures discussed above.

** External suggestions

These links are recommendations only; they have not been imported into Zotero
or assigned invented Bibliotheca citation keys.

- *Omar Antolín Camarena, /A Whirlwind Tour of the World of
  \((\infty,1)\)-categories/* (2013),
  [[https://arxiv.org/abs/1303.4669][arXiv:1303.4669]].
  *Best immediate follow-up.* An intuition-first survey of what these
  categories are, why they are useful, and how they fit into the wider higher
  categorical landscape. It deliberately omits most proofs and technical
  details, making it a natural continuation of this note.
- *John C. Baez and Michael Shulman, /Lectures on \(n\)-Categories and
  Cohomology/*, [[https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0608420][arXiv:math/0608420]].
  Informal lectures connecting groupoids, the homotopy hypothesis, Postnikov
  towers, and cohomology. Especially helpful for developing the geometric
  meaning of higher groupoids beyond the slogan “paths between paths”. The
  annotated bibliography offers additional routes into the subject.
- *Viktoriya Ozornova and Martina Rovelli, /\((\infty,n)\)-categories in
  context/* (2025), [[https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.05969][arXiv:2501.05969]].
  The most direct follow-up to the transition from \((\infty,1)\) to
  \((\infty,n)\): an informal introduction with examples, a discussion of
  different models, and connections to mathematical physics. Read this before
  attempting a technical comparison of models.
- *Tom Leinster, /Higher Operads, Higher Categories/*,
  [[https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0305049][arXiv:math/0305049]].
  A substantial book on operads and higher-dimensional category theory, with
  an introductory chapter aimed at topologists. Useful for the algebraic
  organization of weak composition and coherence, including the motivation
  for unbounded higher categories. It is not primarily a modern
  \((\infty,1)\)-category textbook.

** Suggested order

1. Read *Antolín Camarena* for the broad picture, followed by *Ozornova--Rovelli*
   for the \((\infty,n)\) extension.
2. If the topology is unfamiliar, fill in the relevant parts of *Hatcher*;
   read *Baez--Shulman* alongside it for the higher-groupoid interpretation.
3. Choose a technical \((\infty,1)\) route: *Hinich* for lecture notes, or
   *Riehl--Verity* for model-independent categorical methods. Use
   *Goerss--Jardine* for simplicial foundations and *Lurie* as a deeper reference.
4. For directed higher cells and coherence, branch into *Polygraphs* for
   rewriting and strict models, or *Leinster* for operadic and weak higher
   categorical structures. These are complementary branches, not mandatory
   prerequisites for \((\infty,1)\)-category theory.
