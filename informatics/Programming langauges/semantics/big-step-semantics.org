* The idea

*Big-step semantics defines complete evaluations by inference rules.*
A judgement records an input and a final result; its justification is a
finite derivation tree. The relation is the /least/ one closed under the
rules, not an arbitrary relation satisfying their implications
[cite:@pierceLogicalFoundations2026 chap. Imp, sec. Evaluation as a Relation].
The rules below adapt that presentation to integers and total environments.

We work in \(\mathbf{Set}\). For our arithmetic syntax, recall

\[
F(X)=\mathbb Z+\mathrm{Var}+\mathrm{Op}\times X^2,
\qquad E=\mu F,
\qquad \mathrm{Op}=\{+,-,*\}.
\]

Let \(\mathrm{Env}=\mathrm{Var}\to\mathbb Z\), with total environments.
Write \(\underline n\) for a syntactic numeral and
\(\widehat o:\mathbb Z^2\to\mathbb Z\) for the integer operation
interpreting the syntactic operator \(o\).

* Judgements and rules

The judgement \(\rho\vdash e\Downarrow n\) means that \(e\) evaluates
to \(n\) under \(\rho\). Define it inductively by

\[
\frac{}{\rho\vdash\underline n\Downarrow n}
\qquad
\frac{}{\rho\vdash x\Downarrow\rho(x)}
\]

and, for each \(o\in\mathrm{Op}\),

\[
\frac{
  \rho\vdash e_1\Downarrow n_1
  \qquad
  \rho\vdash e_2\Downarrow n_2
}{
  \rho\vdash e_1\mathbin{o}e_2\Downarrow\widehat o(n_1,n_2)
}.
\]

The premises justify the result; their placement does not, by itself,
specify a temporal order of evaluation. In this pure, total language,
that order does not affect the integer obtained.

* The relation as a least fixed point

Let \(J=\mathrm{Env}\times E\times\mathbb Z\) be the set of possible
judgements. Define the axiom instances

\begin{align*}
B_0={}&
\{(\rho,\underline n,n)\mid\rho\in\mathrm{Env},\ n\in\mathbb Z\}
\\
&\;\cup\;
\{(\rho,x,\rho(x))\mid\rho\in\mathrm{Env},\ x\in\mathrm{Var}\}.
\end{align*}

The rules induce a monotone operator
\(\Phi:\mathcal P(J)\to\mathcal P(J)\):

\begin{align*}
\Phi(R)=B_0\;\cup\;\bigl\{&
  (\rho,e_1\mathbin{o}e_2,\widehat o(n_1,n_2))\mid\\
  &o\in\mathrm{Op},\;
   (\rho,e_1,n_1)\in R,\;
   (\rho,e_2,n_2)\in R\,\bigr\}.
\end{align*}

Thus the big-step relation is

\[
\boxed{B=\mu\Phi=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb N}\Phi^k(\varnothing).}
\]

The union over finite stages suffices because these rules have finitely
many premises. Membership in \(B\) means having a finite derivation;
the iteration index bounds derivation height, not execution steps.

There is an initial-algebra reading here too. Regard
\((\mathcal P(J),\subseteq)\) as a category, with an arrow exactly when
one relation is included in another. Monotonicity makes \(\Phi\) an
endofunctor. A \(\Phi\)-algebra is an inclusion \(\Phi(R)\subseteq R\),
i.e. a rule-closed relation. Since \(B\) is contained in every such
relation, it is the *initial \(\Phi\)-algebra*. This is a different
category and functor from those defining the syntax \(E\). For the general
initial-algebra and fixed-point background, see
[cite:@jacobsIntroductionCoalgebraMathematics2012 secs. 2.4 and 4.5.1].
The poset and judgement-family constructions here are worked out for the
displayed inference rules; see also
[[id:2D0BB075-2A72-4BD2-B116-7ABEBE2AE647][Initial algebra of an endofunctor]].

* Derivations as an inductive type

To retain proofs rather than just their existence, use families of sets
indexed by judgements. For a family \(A=(A_j)_{j\in J}\), define

\[
(H A)_j=
\sum_{r\in\mathcal R_j}\prod_{i=1}^{k_r} A_{p_{r,i}}.
\]

Here \(\mathcal R_j\) consists of instantiated rules concluding \(j\),
and \(p_{r,1},\ldots,p_{r,k_r}\) are the premises of \(r\).
The empty product is \(1\), accounting for axioms. On maps, \(H\) maps
each premise component. The initial algebra in \(\mathbf{Set}^J\) is

\[
\mathrm{Deriv}=\mu H,
\qquad
j\in B\iff\mathrm{Deriv}_j\ne\varnothing.
\]

*\(\mu F\) contains syntax trees; \(\mu H\) contains derivation trees.*
Passing to \(B\) forgets which derivation establishes a judgement.

* For this language, evaluation is also a fold

Take \(D_{\mathrm{rel}}=\mathrm{Env}\to\mathcal P(\mathbb Z)\).
Define \(\alpha_{\mathrm{rel}}:F(D_{\mathrm{rel}})\to D_{\mathrm{rel}}\)
by

\begin{align*}
\alpha_{\mathrm{rel}}(\mathsf{num}(n))(\rho)&=\{n\},\\
\alpha_{\mathrm{rel}}(\mathsf{var}(x))(\rho)&=\{\rho(x)\},\\
\alpha_{\mathrm{rel}}(o,f,g)(\rho)
  &=\{\widehat o(a,b)\mid a\in f(\rho),\ b\in g(\rho)\}.
\end{align*}

Structural induction, using the rules above, gives

\[
\boxed{
\mathsf{fold}_{\alpha_{\mathrm{rel}}}(e)(\rho)
=\{n\mid\rho\vdash e\Downarrow n\}
=\{[\![e]\!]\rho\}.
}
\]

Every result set is therefore a singleton. This fold presentation works
because evaluation recurses on proper subexpressions. For languages with
loops or general recursion, rule premises may instead evaluate the same
program again; a direct structural evaluator need not exist.
See the [[id:f82515f7-7c76-4939-b56c-20244819c7f5][overview]] for the correspondence proofs and scope.
